The problem

You find out how crowded the bus is when it is already in front of you.

01

No warning at the stop

The next bus may have seats free, or no room to stand. You learn which one it is when it pulls up — too late to leave earlier, wait for the next one, or pick another route.

02

Every route has its own rush

Route 717A peaks at 9 in the morning. Another route peaks at 6. A single city-wide "peak hours" warning does not fit any of them well — the crowd has to be read route by route, hour by hour.

03

One bad hour spreads

When a busy route fails, its riders do not vanish. They land on the other routes from the same stops — and today nobody can see where that load will go before it happens.

What we built

A pattern-twin of the bus network, and a planner that thinks in hours.

Three steps: count a real month, replay it as a living day, and plan around what the replay shows.

Step 1

Count a real month

Step 2

The board below plays a typical October day back, hour by hour. Stops swell and calm with their real boarding counts. It is a pattern simulation — what usually happens, not a live feed. It is labelled that way, always.

Step 3

Plan around the crowd

Pick a route and an hour. See the verdict, the full crowding curve, the quiet windows — and for the busiest routes, what happens to the load if that route stops.

How this works — and where it stops

Where the numbers come from. Delhi's bus e-ticketing data for one full month — October 2026: 25,59,899 tickets. We added up the passengers on every ticket by route, by boarding stop, and by hour, and counted the real journeys between stops.

How a band is decided. Each route — and each stop on the twin — is judged against its own day. Its 8 busiest hours are "bahut bheed", the next 8 "khadi bheed", and its 8 quietest "aaram se seat milegi". The band answers the question a daily traveller asks: for this route, is this a busy hour or a quiet one?

How the twin is drawn. The ticket data names stops but carries no coordinates, so the board is a schematic: a layout algorithm places stops close together when many passengers travel between them. It shows the shape of the city's movement, not its geography.

How the what-if works. For the 40 busiest routes, the app can stop a route for one simulated hour. Its boardings from its five busiest boarding stops are shared out to the other routes starting at those stops, in proportion to how many people those routes board there at that hour. Boardings from the route's smaller stops are counted and reported as outside the model. It is a simple, stated model — real riders may also wait, walk, or take the Metro.

What to keep in mind. E-tickets are only about 3–4% of all tickets sold, so this is a sample, not a full count. Everything here is the usual pattern from October 2026. It cannot see today's crowd, a cancelled bus, rain, or a festival day. There are no live bus positions in this data, and this page never pretends otherwise.
